10 Second Challenge

Residuals Normal Distribution Measurement Error Bias vs. Variance

About

Students try to stop a timer as close as possible to exactly 10.00 seconds. Each attempt records a signed error (observed time minus target time), and after several attempts, the app summarises the mean error, best absolute error, and standard deviation of times. This is a simple but effective hook for residuals: every attempt’s error is exactly analogous to a residual in a regression model — the gap between an observed value and a “true” target. With enough attempts pooled across the class, the errors tend to scatter in a roughly bell-shaped pattern around zero, giving a hands-on way to introduce the normal distribution of errors, and to separate two very different kinds of “being wrong”: a biased timer (consistently early or late — a non-zero mean error) versus a noisy one (highly variable, but centred correctly — a large standard deviation with a mean error near zero).

General questions

  • After several attempts, were your errors mostly positive, mostly negative, or a mix of both? What would that pattern suggest about your internal sense of timing?
  • What’s the difference between having a mean error far from zero versus having a large standard deviation of your times? Which one describes “consistently wrong,” and which describes “unpredictable”?
  • If your very first attempt happened to be exact (0.00s error), would that tell you much about how good you are at this task? Why or why not?
  • As a class, pool everyone’s errors together and look at the shape of the distribution. Does it look roughly symmetric and bell-shaped around zero, or is it skewed one way?
  • In a regression model, a “residual” is the gap between what a model predicts and what actually happened. How is your error on this task similar to a residual? How is it different?
  • If someone was consistently early by about 0.5 seconds on every attempt, would practicing more make them more accurate, more precise, both, or neither? What would actually need to change?
  • Where else in real life do we see this same distinction between a biased measurement process and a noisy one (e.g. a scale that’s always off by the same amount, versus one that gives a different reading every time you step on it)?
