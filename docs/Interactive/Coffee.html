Should We Launch It?

Bayesian Inference Beta-Binomial Model Conjugate Priors Sequential Updating

About

This app is a companion piece to The Stolen Laptop, but framed around a business decision instead of a courtroom: should a café launch a new product, based on customer feedback? Students start with a flat Beta(1, 1) prior on the true proportion of customers who genuinely support the launch. Over 6 simulated days, a fresh batch of customer comments arrives each day, and students read through them, checking off which ones they judge as genuine support. That count (say, 6 out of 10 comments) is treated as binomial data and combined with the current prior using the conjugate beta-binomial update: \(\alpha \to \alpha + x\), \(\beta \to \beta + (n - x)\). Because the update rule is just simple addition, this is a great follow-up to the courtroom app for showing students why the beta distribution is used as a prior for proportions in the first place — the math is transparent enough to do by hand, while the app’s ridge plot makes the accumulating evidence visually obvious. The app also lets students copy the day-by-day data directly into R, to double-check the same result using dbeta() or a package like bayesrules.

General questions

  • Why is Beta(1, 1) a sensible “starting point” if you genuinely have no idea whether customers support the launch? What would it mean to start with a different prior instead, like Beta(10, 10) or Beta(2, 8)?
  • The update rule is just \(\alpha \to \alpha + x\) and \(\beta \to \beta + (n - x)\). Why does adding the count of “genuine support” comments to \(\alpha\), and the count of the rest to \(\beta\), make sense conceptually?
  • Does the order in which the 6 days of feedback arrive change the final posterior? Try to reason through this without running the app again — would summing everything at once give the same answer as updating day by day?
  • After 6 days, is the posterior distribution narrower or wider than the Day 1 posterior? What does that tell you about how confident you should be by the end?
  • If Day 1 had unusually strong support (say, 9 out of 10 comments) purely by chance, how much does that early result continue to influence the final posterior after 5 more days of data? Does its influence shrink over time?
  • How is this beta-binomial setup similar to the courtroom app’s evidence-rating process? How is it different — specifically, which one relies on a subjective 0–10 rating, and which one relies on a countable, objective tally?
  • If you were the café owner, at what posterior mean (or what width of posterior distribution) would you feel comfortable actually launching the product? Is there a “right” threshold, or is that a business judgement call layered on top of the statistics?
  • The app lets you copy the daily counts into R. Why might it be useful to double-check an app’s calculations independently, rather than just trusting the visual output?
