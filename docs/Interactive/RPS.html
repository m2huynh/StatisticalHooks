RockPaperScissors

Sampling Law of Large Numbers Randomness Small Samples

About

Students play 10 rounds of Rock Paper Scissors against the computer, watching their running score plotted live as they go. With only 10 games, results swing wildly — a player can be up 4 points after 5 games and end up losing, purely from the small sample size. This is a great hook for discussing why small samples are noisy and unreliable estimators of a “true” underlying rate, and why the Law of Large Numbers only kicks in once you play many, many more rounds. As a twist, the computer opponent isn’t purely random — it quietly adapts based on the outcome of the previous round — which opens up a second discussion thread on detecting non-randomness and pattern-seeking behaviour once students start noticing (or suspecting) a pattern in the computer’s choices.

General questions

  • After 10 games, did your final score feel like a fair reflection of your actual skill against the computer, or could it easily have gone the other way with a bit of luck?
  • If you played another 10 games right now, would you expect a similar result, a completely different one, or something in between? Why?
  • How many games do you think you’d need to play before your win rate started to “settle down” and reliably reflect the true probabilities involved?
  • Did you notice any pattern in the computer’s choices as you played? If you suspected a pattern, how would you go about testing whether it was real or just your imagination?
  • If a friend told you they’d won 8 out of 10 games against the computer and concluded “I’m just better at reading the computer,” what would you want to know before you believed them?
  • As a class, compare everyone’s final scores. Do they all cluster around a similar result, or is there a wide spread? What does that spread tell you about how much weight to put on any one person’s 10-game outcome?
  • Where else in everyday life do people draw big conclusions from a small number of outcomes (a few good trades, a short winning streak, a handful of “lucky” days) that might really just be normal randomness?
