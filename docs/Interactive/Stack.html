Stack Game

Hypothesis Testing Between-Subjects Design Independent Samples t-Test Attention

About

This app is a fast-paced block-stacking game with a hidden research design baked in. Before playing, the instructor will allocate students into one of trwo conditions: Control, the standard game with no interruptions, or Distraction, where a new notification-style pop-up (a text, a low battery warning, a calling gem) appears after every successful stack. Everyone plays the same game with the same mechanics — the only thing that differs between groups is whether they’re being pulled away from the task with simulated phone notifications. This is a clean, self-contained between-subjects design: students self-select (or are randomly assigned by the instructor) into one of two independent groups, and their final scores become the outcome variable for a live hypothesis test comparing means.

General questions

  • Before playing, did you expect the distraction messages to affect your score much? Did the actual experience match that expectation?
  • If the instructor let students freely choose which group (Control or Distraction) to join, could that cause a problem when comparing the two groups afterward? What would you suggest instead?
  • Why is it important that both groups play the exact same game, with only the distractions differing? What would go wrong if the Distraction group also had a different game mechanic?
  • As a class, compare the average scores of the Control group and the Distraction group. Does there appear to be a difference? How confident are you that this difference (if any) reflects a real effect, rather than just random variation between two groups of different people?
  • Some students might get distracted by a notification and actually play better out of a spike in adrenaline or focus. How would that possibility affect how we interpret the results?
  • Outside of this game, where else does a “distraction condition vs. control condition” comparison show up in real research or everyday life (e.g. studying with music on vs. off, driving while texting)?
  • If this experiment were repeated with a much larger class, would you expect the difference between groups to become clearer, or would it stay just as uncertain? Why?
